# Check Writing CERL

Test the links that make a paragraph an argument.

## Purpose

Independently review Claim, Evidence, Reasoning and Link, or Main idea, Evidence,
Analysis and Link back to the central argument. Check their actual
relationships, not labels or a four-sentence pattern. Preserve the native
structure of procedural, descriptive and other nonargumentative units.

## Inputs

Read the [writing contract](../writing-coordinator/references/writing_contract.md).
Load the current brief, relevant MOC and recipient-supplied writing guidance, exact draft
version, section purpose, central argument and its status, evidence sources and
neighboring paragraphs. A paragraph cannot be judged for its link to an
unavailable central argument; mark that limit.

## Steps

1. Identify the unit's job. Is it making an argument, reporting a result,
   describing a procedure or serving another function? Apply CERL to its
   reasoning without forcing all material into argumentative prose.
2. Locate the controlling claim or main idea. State it in one faithful
   sentence. If two unrelated claims compete, identify the split or the
   relationship the author needs to explain.
3. Identify evidence actually supplied for that claim. A citation marker,
   methodological name or assertion of importance is not by itself evidence.
   Point to the finding, example, observation or source passage being used.
4. Identify reasoning or analysis: how the evidence bears on the claim, with
   what assumption or limitation. If the text only repeats the finding, the
   warrant is still missing.
5. Identify the earned link to the section purpose and central argument.
   It may be clear from context rather than a formulaic final sentence, but
   a reader must be able to explain why this unit belongs here.
6. Test the connections in sequence: does the evidence address this claim;
   does the reasoning license this inference; and does the implication follow
   without a new unsupported claim? Note a failed connection even when all
   four kinds of sentence are present.
7. Check adjacent units. Does the next paragraph depend on what was established?
   A transition word cannot repair a missing causal, comparative or inferential
   relation.
8. Reverse-outline the section's main ideas. Look for repeated claims,
   unsupported jumps and paragraphs that never advance the central argument.
   Do not reject an intentional procedural sequence for lacking a thesis.
9. Return the smallest repair: a clearer claim, relevant evidence, an explicit
   warrant, a justified qualification, a better location or a necessary link.
   Leave missing science to the appropriate owner.

## Outputs

For each affected unit:

| Location and job | Claim/main idea | Evidence | Reasoning/analysis | Link to argument | Broken relationship and repair |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Use prose for a small review. Mark nonargumentative units as such with a reason.
Return an overall judgment only for the inspected scope, with unresolved
evidence or central-claim gaps.

## Boundaries

- Do not count sentences or demand the labels C, E, R and L in finished prose.
- Evidence and reasoning can interleave or span a coherent multi-paragraph unit.
- Do not manufacture certainty to make a provisional argument easier to link.
- Do not replace scientific review with a logical-looking paragraph.
- Do not rewrite the whole passage unless rewriting was assigned; provide
  precise revision instructions to its writer.

## Handoffs

Return through writing-coordinator. plan-writing-argument repairs the argument
plan; develop-writing-evidence supplies missing warrants or source anchors;
the section writer revises. verify-writing-citations and
check-writing-substance remain independent checks with distinct purposes.

## Checks

- The review describes relationships, not merely component presence.
- Each local implication is earned and linked to the actual central argument.
- The suggested repair adds meaning without formulaic repetition.
- Procedural exceptions are justified by the passage's function.
